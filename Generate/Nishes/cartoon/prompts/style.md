Style guide for the cartoon_photos niche.

Each segment becomes one still frame that illustrates what the segment says, drawn in the clean 2D editorial comic style below. Write one prompt per segment in English. The task names frame_language as the language for all on-frame text.

# Global Style

Clean, even 2D comic ink outlines in medium-fine black with smooth closed lines. Warm muted earthy palette: pale dusty blue sky, sandy and ochre earth, olive and sage greens, warm medium wood, muted terracotta and mustard accents. Flat fills with at most one simple darker cel shade along the lower edge and an occasional tiny curved highlight tick on glass or liquid.

Charts, maps, diagrams and cards sit on a warm pale cream backdrop. Pure white is reserved for small title plaques, paper label cards and the white head of the main character. Indoor scenes use a flat warm cream wall, a simple counter or wooden table and clean furniture outlines. Keep each scene on one flat plane.

Describe smooth closed ink outlines, flat matte surfaces and simple shapes. Avoid rough wobbly pen lines, cross-hatching, 3D CGI looks, soft airbrush gradients, bokeh, depth of field and realistic photos.

Do not write any closing style summary sentence at the end of a prompt (no sentence that restates the outlines, palette, finish or art style). The style sentence from preset.md is appended automatically after generation, so every prompt ends on its last scene or plaque description.

# Subject and Context

Draw the scene, setting, props and concept from the whole Sentence line, using the Before and After lines to follow the story. The >>marked<< fragment is the primary beat: the frame must show what that fragment says, while the rest of the sentence supplies the setting and objects. A short subtitle fragment rarely shows enough alone, so build the picture from the full sentence.

On-frame text is different: take it only from the Text line of the current segment, never from Before or After. Write it in clean uppercase dictionary form (nominative singular for inflected languages such as Russian). Plaques, label cards and lettering appear only on the analytical layouts described under On-Frame Text and Lettering; ordinary scenes stay plain pictures.

# Visual Diversity and Shot Scale

Choose each shot by the segment's meaning across four independent axes so consecutive frames never fall into a rigid cycle or repetitive template. Adjacent segments must differ on at least two axes, and the exact same layout mode or camera angle must never repeat back to back:

1. **Composition and shot scale** (pick the mode that best conveys the beat):
- Wide establishing scene: a field, farm, factory floor, market or supermarket aisle, with a figure at about 20 percent of image height.
- Medium interaction: a figure at 40 to 60 percent of image height, leaning in from the left or right, seated, walking or handling an object.
- Three-quarter full figure: a character shown from head to toe at a natural diagonal angle in context.
- Foreground-to-background depth: a large concrete prop on one side of the foreground with the main setting or small figure visible behind it on the same flat 2D plane.
- Macro view of hands or objects: two hands peeling a potato, a hand holding a tuber, tuber skins compared side by side. Hands and objects may fill most of the frame; a human face never does.
- Overhead flat view: dishes on a table, sorted tubers in a tray, tools on a workbench, seen from directly above.
- Cross-section or cutaway: a vertical soil cross-section, an enlarged biological vessel, a cut-open tuber with its roots.
- Illustrated flat map: olive and sage landmasses, crisp coastlines, a dashed terracotta route arrow, short uppercase labels.
- Split comparison: the frame divided vertically into two panels, A on one side and B on the other.
- Visual metaphor: a brass balance scale, a winner's podium, a chained padlock, circular process arrows, a spotlight cone over an object, a magnifying glass circle enlarging a detail.
- Diagram, chart or timeline: a warm cream chart card, checklist or clean horizontal timeline.
- Neat arrangement: lined-up crops, shelves stocked with jars, a crate of produce or a small heap on a conveyor belt, kept clean and readable with few tiny items.

2. **Camera angle** (vary naturally across scenes instead of defaulting to flat front-on eye-level):
- Eye-level view, low angle (looking slightly upward to emphasize height or scale), high angle (looking down across a workspace, table or field), top-down bird's-eye view, three-quarter angle, side profile view, and back view or over-the-shoulder view looking past a figure toward an object or landscape.

3. **Subject placement and visual rhythm**:
- Alternate placement across segments: left third, right third, far background, diagonal composition, seen from behind, or character-free object/diagram frames.
- About half or more of all frames work best as pure object, macro, cutaway, map, chart and balance-scale scenes that show objects and diagrams only, with people and personified characters left out. Add a character only when human action, scale, tasting, confusion, farming, factory or lab work, or a clear emotion adds value to the segment.

4. **Setting, time of day and flat 2D light temperature**:
- Vary the flat 2D palette temperature and backdrop context to match the story beat (pale dusty-blue morning sky, warm ochre midday field, amber dusk horizon, indoor cream wall with a simple warm lamp cone) while keeping crisp flat fills, single-layer 2D comic ink outlines and avoiding realistic gradients or bokeh.

Never reuse one fixed shot order or sentence opener. Do not start every prompt with the same phrase, and do not place the character at the center every time. Vary the visual mode from clip to clip: never use the same layout mode for three consecutive segments, such as three medium kitchen scenes in a row, and never alternate overhead and macro shots of the same object back to back. Open each prompt with its subject or camera view, for example "Macro view of two hands ...", "Low-angle view of ...", "Overhead flat view of ...", "Over-the-shoulder view past ...", "Vertical soil cross-section ...", "Brass balance scale on a warm cream backdrop ...", "Split vertically down the center ...". When consecutive segments share one sentence, give each segment a different facet of the same idea.

Close-up views are allowed for hands, feet, food, plants, tools, objects and cutaways: write macro view or tight view for them. Close-ups of faces, heads, eyes and portraits are forbidden: show whole figures at normal scale instead.

# Character Canon

Select the character type with the first matching rule:
1. No characters: maps, charts, balance scales, soil or botanical cross-sections, molecular diagrams, standalone object facts, macro and overhead object views.
2. Personified microbe character: gut microbes and personified crops, organs or objects in a metaphor (competition, cooperation, thirst, mechanical resistance).
3. Cartoon adult figure: a named profession, workplace, laboratory, farm, kitchen, market, history, culture or family.
4. Main character: second-person you, a lone traveller, an unnamed observer, a single person at home, solo cooking or tasting, digestive discomfort, confusion, survival or a human-height scale.
5. Otherwise: no characters, a plain 2D concept visual or card.

Keep one kind of human character in a frame and never mix human and personified characters in one frame. Plain 2D animals may appear with the main character or a cartoon adult figure and count toward the 4-character total. Draw ingredients, tools and surroundings as plain inanimate items. Use the same character canon for the whole video.

a) Default hero, the main character: a large smooth pure-white round head with a crisp medium-fine black ink outline, small solid black dot eyes, expressive thin curved eyebrows, one mouth expression on the face (choosing either a closed-line mouth, a closed-line smile, an open O-shaped mouth, or a worried wavy mouth), and a plain solid-black stick torso (adding a hat, apron or backpack only if the scene calls for it, with thin black stick arms, simple black stick hands and thin black stick legs). It may carry one simple accessory: a backpack, a chef hat and apron, a farmer hat, a hard hat, a shopping basket or a magnifying glass. Repeat this complete description in every prompt where the main character appears, because each frame is generated independently.

Cartoon adult figure: a proportional 2D figure (head about one fifth of body height) with warm natural skin tones, simple solid-colour hair, small black dot eyes, a small curved-line nose, expressive eyebrows, one mouth expression on the face (a closed-line mouth, a closed-line smile, an open O-shaped mouth, or a worried wavy mouth) and proportional clothed limbs. Keep one fixed outfit per role across the whole video: white lab coat and eyeglasses for a scientist; muted rust plaid flannel shirt, medium-blue denim overalls and brown boots for a farmer; muted brown work shirt, tan trousers, brown boots and a tan hard hat for a miner; white jacket and tall white cap for a chef; period tunic and trousers for a historical figure; muted sage sweater and medium-brown trousers for a neutral family member. Repeat the complete face, skin, hair, limb and outfit description of each cartoon adult figure in every prompt where it appears.

Personified character: an active crop, plant, organ part or microbe with small black dot eyes, expressive eyebrows, a closed-line mouth, thin black stick arms and legs, and at most one tiny prop when it enacts a metaphor. Draw every microbe as a personified character rather than plain slime. Keep non-agent tools plain, with no eyes or mouths. Draw real and extinct animals (fish, trilobites, dinosaurs, prehistoric cats, insects, birds, mammals, burrowing animals) as plain 2D animals with neutral animal features.

Show emotion on the face and through posture and action: choose one mouth shape directly on the face (closed-line smile, open O-shaped mouth, or worried wavy mouth), add squinting happy arc eyes for joy, and place comic signs (sweat drops at the temple, a small question mark or exclamation mark above the head) cleanly beside or above the head. Characters stay silent: no speech bubbles, no dialogue quotes, no microphones, and nobody talks to the camera.

# On-Frame Text and Lettering

Ordinary frames carry no on-frame text. Wide, medium, macro, overhead, spotlight, podium and neat arrangement scenes are pure visual illustrations with no plaques, label cards, header bands or lettering, unless the segment's Text line gives an explicit number, date or measurement to show. Decide each segment independently and never repeat a label-card pattern across a batch.

Text belongs to analytical layouts only: maps, charts, timelines, number cards, split A | B comparisons, balance scales and labeled scientific cutaways or diagrams. Use text there only when the current segment's Text line contains the number or date, the proper place, era or name, the compared terms or the scientific term. The five cases are: numbers, dates and measurements (for example 255 MILLION YEARS or 9 M LONG); proper names, eras and places (for example PANGAEA or CARBONIFEROUS); direct two-item comparisons (A | B); map labels; and chart labels.

Each text item is written in frame_language, is 1 to 4 words long, is copied whole from the current segment's Text line and ends on a content word. Keep labels concise: most labels are 1 to 2 words, and 3 to 4 words only for full proper names or measurements. Labels for one object use one word for each part, at most 4 parts, and a frame carries at most four labels. Omit the text when a phrase or an adjective and noun is split across segments. Standalone number, era or concept cards carry exactly one text phrase per frame; comparison, map and chart layouts carry one main title plus the short labels or dates that the layout requires. Verbs, pronouns, prepositions, casual subtitle fragments, bare units such as YEARS AGO, unitless numbers (except four-digit calendar years) and split number-and-unit fragments never appear as text. When a number and its unit are split across adjacent segments, omit the text for that frame. Standalone text-only slides are at most 1 in 5 clips.

Vary the text carrier across the video:
1. Direct bold lettering on the sky or cream backdrop: bold black uppercase lettering centered at the top reading <TEXT>.
2. Full-width header band: a full-width pale cream top header band separated by a thin horizontal black line reading <TEXT> in bold black uppercase lettering.
3. Pointer-arrow label: a curved black ink pointer arrow with clean black uppercase lettering reading <TEXT>.
4. Label card: a white or beige framed label card, or a muted red or gold accent plaque with a crisp black border, reading <TEXT> in bold black uppercase lettering.

Place location words before the word reading, so that they never become part of the text. The text after reading or reads is always followed by in bold black uppercase lettering or ends the sentence with a period or comma, so scene prepositions never follow the label text directly. Empty plaques and blank label cards are forbidden.

Emphasis markers: two crossed bold red ink strokes making a red X over a rejected object; a red warning triangle with an exclamation mark for a hazard; a clean green checkmark for a working choice.

# Positive Phrasing

Describe only what is visible, as a finished positive scene. When something must look absent, describe the visible state instead: a blank dark screen, clean clear air, a bare black stick torso, neutral animal features. Never write a negative phrase that names the unwanted thing, such as one beginning with the word free or lacking, because the image model draws the named object.

Safe replacements for common concepts:
- write image height instead of frame height;
- write curves, makes, shapes, creates, covers or lines instead of forms, forming or formed;
- write stocky tan prehistoric cat with long upper canines and a short tail instead of saber-toothed cat;
- write head angled up, leaning forward or mid-step instead of tilting or moving;
- write around, bordering or enclosing instead of framing;
- describe the exact limb pose instead of gesture;
- write cluster, clump, grove, stand or pair instead of group, groups, row of or rows of for trees, rocks, towers and other objects, and write lined-up for crops;
- write two lines meeting at a corner instead of axes, or write X and Y axes only on a labeled chart card;
- write round glass disc or magnifier instead of lens.

Forbidden prompt words to exclude from every prompt: parchment, flag, passport, form, forms, forming, formed, framing, frame height, uniform, emblem, crest, knife, saber, saber-toothed, gesture, gestures, tilting, moving, pan, zoom, track, vector, bold outline, thick outline, heavy outline, sketch, sketchy, 16:9, widescreen, no, not, never, none, render, rendered, rendering, 4k, 8k, uhd, photorealistic, hyperrealistic, masterpiece, trending, octane, unreal engine, lens, 35mm, anamorphic, aperture, focal length, film grain, film still, without, nothing, nobody, cannot, group, groups, crowd, crowds, row of, rows of, portrait.

# Safety and Content Rules

Show adults only; children, babies and young people are forbidden. Weapons are forbidden in every frame: use a vegetable peeler, wooden spoon, spatula or garden trowel instead of blades. Blood, wounds, injuries, bruises, physical pain or agony, corpses and any harm, mistreatment or cruelty to animals are strictly forbidden: whenever a segment touches on illness, danger or harsh conditions, show intact unharmed subjects, a clean educational diagram, or a calm visual metaphor (such as a warning triangle or balance scale) instead. Speech is silent: write the expression itself (smile, squint, O-shaped mouth, worried mouth) and never a speech verb, shouted word, or a talking mouth.

Include at most 4 characters in total per frame, counting humans and animals together. When two to four adults appear, describe each adult one by one, or write two adult characters or three adult characters. Avoid plural group nouns such as people, men, women, farmers, workers, villagers, crowds or groups.

Object arrangements are allowed when they are neat and readable: lined-up crops, shelves stocked with jars, a crate of produce, a small heap on a conveyor belt. Keep each kind of object to one clear grouping and avoid clutter of tiny items.

Every frame shows at least one concrete foreground focal object, diagram, cutaway or character; bare empty sky-and-ground landscapes are forbidden. When a segment names absence or an abstract concept (empty land, heat, extinction, an era, a transition), show a concrete object, cross-section, thermometer, fossil, clock, compass or icon with a clear visual marker, or keep the main character examining a concrete object from the segment. When a segment names a body part, show the whole character pointing or touching that part; hands alone appear only in a macro view of handling an object.

Exclude @handles, watermarks, video player UI, random ducks or ponds unless the segment names them, and mixed-language garbage text.

When consecutive segments share the same subject, vary at least two elements from frame to frame: the layout, the focal object or prop, the character pose or action, and the setting. Avoid repeated identical cards or repeated identical backdrops.

# Choosing the Frame

Pick the layout by the segment's main idea, checking in this order:
1. Direct comparison or contrast: split panels, a balance scale or an equals sign with a red X.
2. Process, sequence, cycle or a choice of paths: two to four linked mini-illustrations with clean black arrows.
3. Quantities, a chart or a criteria list: a warm cream chart card with bars or curve lines and short labels, or a checklist with green checkmarks.
4. Geography, origin or a trade route: an illustrated flat map.
5. Underground growth, roots or internal biology: a cross-section or cutaway.
6. Dates, numbers, eras or a time span: a number card, or a clean horizontal timeline with one active step in muted red.
7. Daily life, survival, cooking, farming, lab work, a market or a personified metaphor: a wide or medium scene with a character, following the character canon.
8. Standalone concept: a concrete focal object or characteristic landscape on a soft ground shadow.

# Final Check

Before returning each prompt, verify every item: one finished scene with a concrete visible focal object, diagram, cutaway or character; the shot scale and opener differ from the previous prompt; a character appears only when the character canon selects it, with at most 4 characters in total; the complete visual description of every main character, cartoon adult figure and personified character is written in full inside this prompt; text appears only in the five allowed cases, copied from the current segment's Text line, in frame_language, in uppercase, 1 to 4 words per item; the prompt uses positive wording and contains no forbidden words and no quotation marks; no closing style summary sentence is written at the end.
